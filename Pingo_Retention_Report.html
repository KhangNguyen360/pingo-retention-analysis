What makes a new user come back?

We analyzed 7,500 Pingo users — tracking what they did on their first day, what they talked about, and whether they returned after 7 days. The findings point clearly to one thing: the depth of the first experience matters far more than where users came from.

"Users who had a real voice conversation on Day 1 came back at nearly twice the rate of users who only sent short text messages."

Finding 01

Half of all users don't come back after Day 1

Of every 100 people who sign up for Pingo, about 50 never return. That's the biggest drop in the whole funnel — bigger than Day 7 churn, bigger than failed paid conversion. It means the onboarding experience is where Pingo wins or loses most of its users.

Key takeaway

The most impactful thing Pingo can do is reduce Day 1 drop-off. Users who come back on Day 1 retain at 57% on Day 7 — compared to only 34% for those who don't. Keeping someone engaged for just one more interaction on their first day roughly doubles the chance they're still around a week later.

Finding 02

Where users come from barely matters

It's tempting to think the right marketing channel is the key to better retention. The data says otherwise. Whether a user came from search, TikTok, a campus campaign, or a creator partnership, their Day 7 retention rates are nearly identical — within 4 percentage points of each other.

Figure 2 — The dashed line is the overall average (45.7%). The gap between best and worst channel is just 4pp.

What this means

Pouring budget into "better" channels won't move the retention needle. A user from TikTok and a user from search behave almost identically once they're in the app. The product experience after signup is what determines retention — not the acquisition source.

Finding 03

What users do on Day 1 predicts everything

This is the core finding of the analysis. Early behaviors — specifically whether users had a voice conversation and whether they came back for a second session — are dramatically more predictive of Day 7 retention than any demographic or acquisition signal.

Figure 3 — Each bar shows Day 7 retention for users who did that thing on Day 1. The dashed line is the baseline average.

The big number

Users who had 2 or more sessions on Day 1 retained at 73.9% — that's 1.7× the baseline and the single strongest signal in the entire dataset. The pattern is clear: one interaction is a visit. Two interactions is a habit forming.

Finding 04

Longer conversations = more likely to return

The length of a user's first session is a reliable signal of how engaged they were. Short sessions under 2 minutes — probably someone poking around without really connecting — lead to below-average retention. Sessions over 5 minutes consistently outperform.

Figure 4 — Day 7 retention climbs as first session depth increases. The biggest jump is between 2–5 min and 5–10 min.

Design implication

The goal isn't to make sessions artificially long — it's to make sure users have a complete, satisfying first interaction rather than a shallow one. A 5-minute voice conversation where something real is said is more valuable than a 15-minute scroll through features.

Finding 05

What people talk about first

The most common first topic users bring to Pingo is school stress — nearly 1,600 users started there. Relationships and motivation lead on Day 7 retention. Sleep is both less common and the lowest-retention topic.

Figure 5 — Topic differences are modest (about 6pp spread), but the pattern still has product implications.

A note on sensitive topics

106 users in this dataset were flagged for crisis language, and many more opened with loneliness or relationship distress. These topics must never be used to target users or optimize for retention. They should only inform how Pingo responds — with more care, better tone, and clear pathways to human support if needed.

Finding 06

The model confirms: voice is the #1 signal

A simple predictive model (logistic regression) was trained to predict which users would return on Day 7. It reached 64.5% accuracy and a ROC-AUC of 0.70 — decent for a first pass. More importantly, it confirmed which early behaviors matter most.

Figure 6 — Model coefficients. The larger the bar, the stronger the predictor. Voice usage (1.033) dwarfs everything else.

Important caveat

Voice usage is correlated with retention — but we can't say it causes it yet. It's possible that more motivated users simply choose voice. The only way to know for sure is to run an A/B test where some users are actively guided toward voice during onboarding, and measure what happens. That experiment would be the highest-value next step.

Recommendations

Three things Pingo should do

These recommendations follow directly from the data. Each one targets a specific gap identified in the analysis.

1
Guide new users into one real voice conversation during onboarding

Voice users retain at 55.6% vs 29.4% for text-only users — a nearly 2× difference. Right now, users can complete onboarding without ever trying voice. That's a missed opportunity. The first onboarding prompt should feel like the start of a real conversation, not a product tour.

What to build
A single voice prompt step in onboarding — optional but clearly encouraged. Low-pressure topic, not a demo.
How to measure it
Voice activation rate on Day 1 · Day 7 retention split by voice vs no-voice cohorts
Watch out for
Don't pressure users to share anything emotional or personal upfront. The prompt should feel safe and easy to answer.
2
Send a gentle second-session nudge within 6 hours of signup

The biggest gap in the entire dataset is between users who had one session (43.4% Day 7 retention) and users who had two or more (73.9%). That 30-point difference is enormous. One well-timed nudge — not pushy, just a friendly "come back" moment — could meaningfully shift this.

What to build
A lightweight in-app or push notification ~4 hours after first session. Topic-agnostic copy.
How to measure it
Second-session rate within 24h · Day 7 retention for second-session vs single-session cohorts
Watch out for
The notification must never reference what was discussed in the first session. No emotional hooks.
3
Track retention by behavior — not just by total numbers

The team probably already tracks overall Day 7 retention. But the real insight is in the breakdown: voice vs no-voice, one session vs two, session under 2 min vs over 5 min. Those splits tell you what's working. Without them, you can't know if a retention improvement came from better onboarding or just a lucky cohort.

What to build
Weekly retention dashboard segmented by voice activation, session count, and session depth.
How to measure it
Voice activation rate · second-session rate · Day 7 retention by each early behavior cohort
Watch out for
Topic-level data should only appear in aggregate. No user-level conversation tracking in dashboards.

Context

What to keep in mind

Limitations of this analysis

  • This is simulated data — real Pingo numbers may look different
  • Everything here is correlation, not causation
  • We only measured Day 7 — Day 30 and Day 90 matter too
  • Topic labels are simplified; real NLP would be richer
  • No time-of-day or device signal included

What I'd do with more time

  • Run an A/B test on voice-first onboarding
  • Build cohort retention curves by signup week
  • Use real conversation embeddings for topic clustering
  • Model Day 30 and Day 90 retention separately
  • Build a crisis-flag escalation pathway

Bottom line

The data tells a simple story: users who have a real, meaningful first day on Pingo come back. Users who have a shallow one don't. The most important thing Pingo can build right now isn't a new feature — it's an onboarding experience that helps every new user have one genuine conversation, and then brings them back for a second one.
